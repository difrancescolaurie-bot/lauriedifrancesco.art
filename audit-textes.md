### Bio

**EN** (hardcodée dans about.astro — version anglaise seule)

Lead :
> Laurie Di Francesco is a photographer and writer. Her work moves between the documentary and the poetic, and always returns to the same question: what holds, when the ground gives way. Thresholds, displacements, bodies forced to reinvent their footing.

Origins :
> Raised between France and Italy, she learned early the strength and fragility of thresholds, and came to question what it means to be rooted — a displacement that reaches all the way into the language she thinks in. The blue of the sky, the same from one end of the earth to the other, runs through her work as a possible anchor point.

Learning :
> As a young adult, she moved to Brazil, a country of the heart and of expansion for her practice. Largely self-taught, she took her first photography courses at twenty in Rio de Janeiro, at Ateliê da Imagem. She later trained at ENSP in Arles, and refined her practice through workshops with Fabienne Pavia, Antoine d'Agata, and Klavdij Sluban, the latter in Slovenia. The rest, she learned by making images.

Approach :
> Hers is a photography of the intimate. She works as close as possible to what is fragile, from within her subjects rather than at a distance from them. The conditions of making are part of the images: what the body could not choose, what chance decided, what the tools recorded on their own.
> A philosophy graduate, she reads Nietzsche, Ricœur and Bachelard, and works from a single conviction: the real can be transfigured without being idealized, in what it carries of light as much as fragility. Her images do not claim to transcribe the world. An image is always an interpretation, and she works this gap openly — with artefacts, imperfection, and a share of chance, never as an absence of mastery but as a way of asking what a photograph is really showing.

Work :
> Her first solo exhibition, Spilling Beyond the Lines, was held in Belo Horizonte in 2024. It brought together twenty years of photographs taken before she called herself a photographer, and looked for the thread running through them. Two bodies of work are ongoing: Quando sono qui, non sono là, on divided presence and what a childhood carries, and a cycle on the body and water, with En Corps à la Mer and La Mer en Corps.
> She is a member of Monolit, the collective founded by Oriane Ciantar Olive, where she works alongside other photographers to share resources, readings and ways of thinking, and to keep the practice moving.

## Projet : La Mer en Corps

### Texte du projet

**EN** (hardcodé dans la page EN)

Paragraphe 1 :
> En Corps à la Mer ended where the body found water again. La Mer en Corps begins one year later, at the other end of that gesture: fifty-three swims along the coast, from August 2025 to July 2026.

Paragraphe 2 :
> A body that had given way, returning to the same water until something in it changed shape. Not healing. Something closer to what a person tells themselves when they are not yet well but cannot afford to collapse: nothing is final. You repeat the movement to hold yourself up, and one day the movement and the water hold you up in return.

[CITATION] Benjamin Clementine
> Repetition is not always about saying the same thing twice. Sometimes it is about returning to a wound until it changes shape.

Intro citation :
> In concert, musician Benjamin Clementine stops the music and has the room repeat that line back to him, until the sentence carries the ones who carry it.

Paragraphe 3 :
> Close to fifty thousand arm strokes. Water from fourteen degrees to twenty-seven. A heart at one hundred and thirty-seven. The year is recorded by a watch on my wrist, one point per second: latitude, longitude, temperature, pulse.

Paragraphe 4 :
> This is what interests me here. Swimming takes everything: no free hand, no free eye, no distance from the subject. Many times I find myself immersed in a deep blue, the exact same blue as the sky on a pure, beautiful day: my landmarks and my sense of direction vanish.

Paragraphe 5 :
> So the recording is handed over. On one side, the most connected tools available: a satellite constellation, a sensor on my wrist, an algorithm that turns a year of positions into a drawing. On the other, the oldest and the most makeshift: a roll of film, a camera sealed inside a waterproof phone pouch, tied to the buoy. Neither gives me control. The first records without me; the second lets in salt, glare, and whatever the swell decides.

Paragraphe 6 :
> What comes back is data, pixels, exposed silver, and most of the time blind attempts, before any of it is a photograph. I have stopped treating that blindness as a failure. It is a condition of the work: at that distance from the shore, with both arms taken, chance is not what ruins the image, it is what makes it. Turning any of this back into an image means passing through every tool that made it, from the algorithm to the developing tank. I did not want to keep any of them out of the frame.

Paragraphe 7 :
